Risk-neutral pricing, diffusion, and quantum mechanics are deep but disjoint topics.

Each topic had highly nontrivial things to say, and I was vaguely aware of them being somehow connected with each other. The original motivation for the Jane Street intern class, and this post, was to clearly understand the interplays. Tl; dr of interesting claims:
1. The <span class="maroon">**risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q(s)$ is fundamentally "how much is a future dollar in scenario $s$ worth today"?**</span>
2. By induction on a single-step binary stump, we can price options along a discrete binary tree via **risk-neutral arbitrage**.
3. The heat diffusion equation is ~energy mass smearing along microscopic Brownian motion.
4. Taking the continuum (Brownian) limit of trees, we clarify how <span class="maroon">**options price evolve as "heat-diffusion" in reverse-time**</span>.
4. The equivalence between options pricing via two "local" and "global" approaches is a special case of **Feynman-Kac**, which describes a <span class="maroon">pairing between *local stochastic differential equation* and *global expectation along a measure*</span>.
5. This local-generator v. global-measure duality also shows up as **Schrodinger equation $\leftrightarrow$ Feynman path integral** in free-particle quantum mechanics via an imaginary time-rotation.

## Discrete options pricing

We begin with the core intuition of **pricing via arbitrage**.

### Pricing a call on a stump

Consider a <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">strike-100 call</strong> on Starbucks (SBUX), whose stock is worth \$100 today. Tomorrow, SBUX goes to \$90 or \$120.

If SBUX goes to \$120, our <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">call</strong> is worth \$20; otherwise it's worth \$0.

The first question to ask is, **how much is this call worth today**?

<RiskNeutralPricing><span slot="probability">$p$</span></RiskNeutralPricing>

From an expectation standpoint, the <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">call</strong> is worth $V = 20p + 0\bar p = 20p$ today. However, I claim that the arbitrage fair is $20/3$ dollars, **independent of the stock probability** (measure).

To see this, consider the following construction. The "no matter how the underlying moves" part is where the name "risk-neutral" comes from.

:::proposition[replicating portfolio]{#prp-replicating-portfolio}
A <strong class="text-[#765298] dark:text-[#c0a1d2]">replicating portfolio</strong> of (2/3 SBUX share and -\$60 cash) exactly replicates the <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">strike-100 call</strong>'s payout in both cases, <strong class="maroon">no matter whether SBUX goes up or down</strong>

<details>
<summary>Payouts</summary>

If SBUX goes to 120, (\$80 share - \$60 cash) = \$20, equal to the call's payout.

If SBUX goes to 90, (\$60 share - \$60 cash) = \$0, equal to the call's payout.

</details>
:::

:::proposition[arbitrage pricing]
When $A$ pays the same as $B$ in all future scenarios, then the current price of $A$ should equal the price of $B$.
:::

As a corollary, the current price of the <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">strike-100 call</strong> should equal that of the <strong class="text-[#765298] dark:text-[#c0a1d2]">replicating portfolio</strong> of $2/3$ SBUX share and -\$60 cash, worth $(2/3)\cdot100-60=20/3$ dollars today.

:::remark[key takeaways]
Pricing a stump is **independent of the real-world measure $\mathbb P$**.
The crux of arbitrage pricing a derivative is constructing a replicating portfolio that replicates *payout in all cases*
:::

### Forshadowing the risk-neutral measure

We introduce $\mathbb Q$ by looking at two more derivatives:
1. A <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">strike-100 call</strong>
2. A <strong class="text-[#c27618] dark:text-[#f2b35f]">strike-100 future</strong>
3. A <strong class="text-[#9a3642] dark:text-[#d9909a]">strike-110 put</strong>

It's not too hard to work out the replicating portfolios using similar construction in :ref[prp-replicating-portfolio] and their [fair price](#risk-neutral-measure--probabilities).
<RiskNeutralMeasure>
  <span slot="up-probability">$p$</span>
  <span slot="down-probability">$\bar p$</span>
  <span slot="up-risk-neutral">$q=1/3$</span>
  <span slot="down-risk-neutral">$\bar q=2/3$</span>
  <span slot="call-portfolio">100-call: (2/3 SBUX, -\$60 cash)<br />$V=(2/3)\cdot100-60=20/3$</span>
  <span slot="future-portfolio">100-fut: (1 SBUX, -\$100 cash)<br />$V=100-100=0$</span>
  <span slot="put-portfolio">110-put: (-2/3 SBUX, \$80 cash)<br />$V=(-2/3)\cdot100+80=40/3$</span>
</RiskNeutralMeasure>

Is it really a coincidence that the fair derivative value today is a linear combination of future payouts according to $q=1/3$ and its complement?

Well, not really: consider two basis-vector like constructions
- An <strong class="text-[#777b3c] dark:text-[#c0c582]">up-dollar</strong> which pays \$1 when SBUX goes to \$120.
- A <strong class="text-[#a65a60] dark:text-[#dca0a5]">down-dollar</strong> which pays \$1 only when SBUX goes to \$90.

Treating them as derivatives, [their fair value work out to](#risk-neutral-measure--state-dollar-portfolios) $q=1/3$ and $\bar q=2/3$, respectively. We'll show later that them being nonnegative and adding up to 1 is not a coincidence.
:::proposition[linear combination]{#prp-linear-combination}
Given any derivative in the setting above defined by payouts $V_{t+1}(\uparrow)$ and $V_{t+1}(\downarrow)$, its fair value today is
$$
\begin{aligned}
  V_t &= V_{t+1}(\uparrow) \cdot \operatorname{fair}(\text{up-dollar}) + V_{t+1}(\downarrow) \cdot \operatorname{fair}(\text{down-dollar}).
\end{aligned}
$$
:::

### From stumps to binomial trees

We intuit the Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing (FTAP) by considering a two-step setup:
1. At $t=0$, stock price is 100
2. For each consecutive time, the stock price could jiggle up or down by \$1.

How to price a <strong class="text-green-700 dark:text-green-400">$t+2$ strike-100 call</strong>?

<BinomialPricing />

Well, we know how to price stumps! The tree's made up of [binomial stumps which have local](#binomial-pricing--base-stump) $q = 1/2$, i.e. fair at $t$ is just the average of fairs at $t+1$.

Already, here're two ways to price at $t=0$, they sound trivially the same now, but correspond to the two faces of Feynmann-Kac when we go to the continuum limit:
1. **Local back-induction**: work out fairs at $t$ from known fairs / payouts at $t+1$ using no-arbitrage conditions.
2. **Global expectation**: recognizing the independent product of tree units with $q=1/2$, price $V_0 = \mathbb E_{\mathbb Q}[V_2]$ via
$$
  \mathbb Q(\uparrow \uparrow) = \mathbb Q(\uparrow \downarrow) = \mathbb Q(\downarrow \uparrow) = \mathbb Q(\downarrow \downarrow) = (1/2)^2 = 1/4
$$

Both methods extend to $n$-step binomial trees.
More generally, in the tree case, $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$ will be generalized to $s \in S = \{\uparrow \uparrow \uparrow \dots, \dots, \uparrow \uparrow \downarrow, \dots, \downarrow \downarrow \downarrow \dots\}$, but it's not hard to convince ourselves of the following:

:::theorem[fundamental theorem of asset pricing; risk-neutral measure]
Define $\mathbb Q(s\in S) = \mathrm{fair}(s)$, the arbitrage fair value of a scenario-$s$ dollar, a basis derivative which pays out 1 under trajectory $s$ and $0$ otherwise. The set of values $\{\mathbb Q(s)\}$ satisfies:

1. $\mathbb Q(s) \geq 0$, because a probabilistic-dollar tomorrow cannot be worth negative today.
2. $\sum_s \mathbb Q(s) = 1$, because one guaranteed dollar tomorrow is worth the same today.

In other words, <span class="maroon">**$\mathbb Q$ is a valid probability measure**</span>. This is the risk-neutral measure.
:::

:::proposition[arbitrage pricing under the risk-neutral measure]
[:ref[prp-linear-combination]](#prp-linear-combination) can be generalized to $V_t = \mathbb E_{s\sim \mathbb Q}V_T(s)$, where $V_T(s)$ is the payout under scenario $s$.

Generalizing this yields the slogan "derivative fair value is a martingale under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q$".
:::

## To the continuum

### Proper limit of binomial trees: Brownian process.

The binomial stump $k \to k\pm 1$ is a "coarse-grained" description of a process that's finer in both price and time granularity.

The key design question is: **what property do we want to keep invariant across scaling**?

A naive two-step binomial tree has $\mathrm{Var}=2$ across $t=2$, and a $n$-step binomial tree has $\mathrm{Var}=n$ across $t=n$.

To get a meaningful "fine-grained continuous limit" of the binomial stump with $\mathrm{Var}=1$ across $t=1$, we take a $n$-step tree and:
- Rescale time interval from $1$ to $\Delta t = 1/n$.
- Rescale value variation from $1$ to $\Delta P = \sqrt{1/n}$.

The limit of this rescaling is the Brownian (Weiner) process.
Its key properties are **Gaussian marginal** and **independent increments** ([see previous post](/blogs/machine-learning/ot-generative-3-diffusion/#brownian-motion-as-score-flow)); in fact, these are also sufficient properties to pin down the Brownian process.
$$
  (x_T \mid \mathbb Q, x_0) \sim \mathcal N(x_0, T)
$$
Intuitively, the Brownian process describes particles undergoing random "jiggles." The macroscopic behavior is them smearing out according to Gaussian averaging.

### Global measure perspective on options pricing

Under the simple binomial-tree-limit model (Brownian process), the fair value at time $t$ is just the expectation of the final payout $V_T$ under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb Q$
$$
  V_t(x_t) = \mathbb E_{x_T\sim \mathbb Q\mid x_t} V_T(x_T)
$$
But we know that $\mathbb Q$ is just Brownian, and so the marginal is Gaussian!

:::proposition[derivative pricing in the Bachelier model]
Under a simplified stock model where:
1. For each $\Delta t = 1/n$, the stock could move up or down by $\sqrt{1/n}$.
2. Take the limit $n\to \infty$.

Given any derivative with payouts $V_T(x_T)$, its arbitrage-enforced fair price at time $t$, given stock price $x$, given by the "smeared out" payout $V_T\ast \mathcal N(0, t)$.
$$
  V_t(x) = \mathbb E_{x_T\sim \mathcal N(0, t)} V_T(x_T) = \left[
    V_T\ast \mathcal N(x, t)
  \right](x)
$$
Note that, again, we didn't specify or depend on the real-world stock measure $\mathbb P$!

(somewhat abusing the pdf notations here; we're also putting everything in natural units and ignoring interest rates).
:::

### Quick detour on the heat equation

This jiggling-smearing picture also featured prominently when physicists started to study heat. Intuitively, heat conduction has the following properties:
- If neighbors are warmer $\implies$ get warm.
- If neighbors are cooler $\implies$ get cool.
- If neighbors are equally warm / cool $\implies$ stay.

The **simplest mathematical formalization** of this intuition turns out to be the heat equation
$$
  \pd t u = \df 1 2 \pd x^2 u \equiv \df 1 2 \nabla^2 u
$$
<HeatCurvature />

{/*
[three-panel animation without dividers below, left shows up-curving parabola with $\dot u \propto \nabla^2 u > 0, middle shows three straight lines all with $\nabla^2 u = 0$, and right shows downward parabola with $\nabla^2 < 0$. Only need to show the \dot u propto for the middle plot]
*/}

:::proposition[interpretation of the heat equation]
The macroscopic behavior described by the heat equation can be induced if we interpret "little irreducible packets of energy (heat)" undegoing random Brownian-motion jiggling. Under the [equivalence with score flow](/blogs/machine-learning/ot-generative-3-diffusion/#brownian-motion-as-score-flow)
$$
  dX_t = dW_t \cong dX_t = \left(-\df 1 2 \nabla \log \rho\right) dt
$$
Combining with the local continuity equation $\pd t \rho + \nabla \cdot (\rho v) = 0$ yields the heat equation
$$
  \pd t \rho = -\nabla \cdot (\rho v) = -\df 1 2 \nabla \cdot (\rho \nabla \log \rho) = \df 1 2 \nabla^2 \rho
$$
:::

### Local arbitrage perspective and the heart of Black-Scholes

This is the crux of the continuous-limit options pricing narrative.
We take the continuous limit of the "backward induction" pricing perspective and demonstrate equivalence with reverse-time heat diffusion. Begin by defining some quantities:
- **Initial condition** denote the terminal derivative payout given terminal stock price $p$ by $V_T(p)$
- Let $V_{p, t}$ denote the derivative's fair price at time $t$ given current price $p$.
- Our argument concerns a replicating portfolio consisting of stock and cash; let $S_{p, t}$ denote the amount of stock in the replicating portfolio.

<LocalArbitrage />

{/*
[binomial branch showing $p / V_{p, t} -> (p+dp / V_{p+dp, t+dt} and c.c.)]
*/}

:::proposition[risk-neutral condition determines $S$ in terms of $V$]
The definition of a risk-neutral portfolio is that locally, the variation in its payout must offset that of the underlying stock move.
This means that stock ownership must exactly "hedge" the payout variation.
$$
  \Delta V_{p, t} \equiv V_{p+dp} - V_{p-dp} = S\cdot 2\, dp
$$
Taking the continuous limit of the finite difference yields
$$
  S = \pd p V
$$
:::
This explain, as a collary, **the options delta $\pd p V$ is the amount of stock in the arbitrage portfolio**.

## Extensions

### Bells and whistles, and the full Black-Scholes

So far, we've done simplifications to focus on the "crux" of the problem, as physicists do.

Starting from the additive-volatility Bachelier model on the left, we derived the reverse-time heat equation on the right:
$$
  dP_t = dW_t \implies \pd t V + \df 1 2 \nabla^2 V = 0
$$

1. If we _explicitly restore volatility_ (diffusion constant):
$$
  dP_t = \sigma\, dW_t \implies \pd t V + \df {\sigma^2}{2} \nabla^2 V = 0
$$
2. Add back interest rate discounting: 1 dollar today is worth $e^{rt}$ time $t$ from now; this shows up as a price-dependent drift term in the dynamics on the left
$$
  dP_t = rP_t\, dt + \sigma\, dW_t \implies \pd t V + rp\pd p V + \df{\sigma^2}{2} \nabla^2 V - rV = 0
$$
3. Use geomtric, instead of additive, Brownian motion.
$$
  dP_t = rP_t\, dt + \sigma P_t\, dW_t \implies \pd t V + rp\pd p V + \df{\sigma^2p^2}{2} \nabla^2 V - rV = 0
$$

Now, there's a clear view of the Nobel-prize winning equation with clear attribution from where each term comes from.
